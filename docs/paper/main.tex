\section{Data}
\subsection{Challenge Dataset}
The training set holds 4{,}167{,}797 movement records covering the whole of 2025, of
which 2{,}085{,}047 are departures. The ranking set holds 431{,}843 records from January
and July 2026, 215{,}876 of them departures, and those are the rows to be predicted.

Ten airports appear in the data: EDDF, EDDM, EGLL, EHAM, LEBL, LEMD, LFPG, LIRF, LSZH and
LTFM. LTAI is named in the challenge description but occurs in no row of either set.

The ranking set does not cover the same airports in both months. January contains all ten
and accounts for 71\% of the rows; July contains only EDDF, EGLL and EHAM. Any validation
scheme that treats the two months symmetrically therefore over-weights July by a wide
margin, and Section~\ref{sec:validation} returns to this.

For departure rows the ranking set blanks two fields, the off-block time
(\texttt{BLOCK\_TIME\_UTC\_mvt}) and the taxi-out time itself. Everything else survives:
the take-off instant, the stand, the runway, the aircraft type, the scheduled time, and
every arrival record untouched. The traffic surrounding a departure is thus fully
observable, which is a direct consequence of the post-operations framing of the problem.

Taxi-out averages 991\,s (16.5 minutes) with a standard deviation of 546\,s. Per-airport
medians span a factor of nearly two, from 711\,s at LSZH to 1{,}319\,s at EGLL.

The distribution is severely heavy-tailed, and this dominates everything that follows.
Ranked by squared deviation from the mean, the ten worst departures out of two million
carry 13.6\% of the total, the worst hundred carry 35.8\%, and the worst ten thousand,
0.48\% of the rows, carry 53.9\%.

\subsection{Data Quality}
The identity \texttt{MVT\_TIME} $-$ \texttt{BLOCK\_TIME} $=$ \texttt{TAXITIME} holds
exactly on every training departure, so the three fields are internally consistent and
timestamps are recorded to the second throughout.

The categorical fields required no normalisation whatsoever. Across nine of them,
including 1{,}899 stand identifiers and 269 aircraft types, not one value differs from
another only by case or surrounding whitespace. Unseen categories are similarly rare:
twenty stands in the ranking set never occur in training, covering 0.106\% of its rows,
and three aircraft types covering 0.005\%.

The labels are another matter. 388 training departures carry a taxi-out of zero or less
and 584 exceed two hours, of which 69 exceed six. Of the 103 above two hours that the
Network Manager also recorded, 96 (93.2\%) have a network off-block time implying a
plausible taxi-out, which locates the error in the airport feed's block time rather than
in the taxi-out being genuinely twelve hours long.

Section~\ref{sec:outliers} reports what happens when those rows are removed, which is
not what one would expect.

\subsection{The Network Manager Off-Block Time}
\label{sec:aobt}
\texttt{AOBT\_3\_flt} is the Network Manager's own record of the off-block instant, an
independent measurement of the same event the challenge blanks. It is present for 98.5\%
of departures and, critically, \emph{it is not blanked in the ranking set}.

Take-off minus this time is therefore a free estimate of taxi-out available on both sides
of the competition, and it is by a wide margin the best of the available handles:

\subsection{The Departure Queue, Counted Rather Than Proxied}
\label{sec:surface}
Since Idris et al. (2002), the number of aircraft already queueing for departure has been
the single strongest predictor of taxi-out time, and it is also the awkward one: an
operational system must forecast it, and the published proxies are counts of movements
within a fixed window, which measure a \emph{flow} where the theory asks for a
\emph{stock}. Thirty departures in the preceding half hour mean something quite different
when they are all still on the surface than when they have all left.

Section~\ref{sec:aobt} removes the difficulty. With both the off-block and the take-off
instant observable for 98.5\% of departures on both sides of the competition, the stock
is a subtraction of two running counts,
\begin{equation}
Q(t) \;=\; \bigl|\{j : b_j \le t\}\bigr| \;-\; \bigl|\{j : d_j \le t\}\bigr| ,
\end{equation}
where $b_j$ and $d_j$ are the off-block and take-off instants of departure $j$. Evaluated
at a flight's own off-block instant, $Q(b_i) - 1$ is the queue it joined; evaluated at its
take-off, it says whether that queue was clearing or still building. Both are computed
airport-wide and per runway, since a busy airport with this flight's runway idle is not a
busy runway.

\subsection{Measuring the Effect Instead of Naming the Cause}
Meteorological features encode \emph{causes} of surface delay: freezing precipitation, low
visibility, a de-icing proxy. The model is then left to infer the effect. The same
observability makes the effect directly measurable instead.

For departure $i$, let $\hat{\tau}_i = d_i - b_i$ be the free taxi-out estimate and
$\tau^{(10)}_{a,r}$ the tenth percentile over its airport and runway, the same statistic
EUROCONTROL's own indicator uses as the unimpeded baseline. Then
\begin{equation}
E_i \;=\; \frac{1}{|W_i|}\sum_{j \in W_i}
          \bigl(\hat{\tau}_j - \tau^{(10)}_{a_j, r_j}\bigr),
\qquad
W_i = \{\, j \ne i : d_i - \Delta < d_j \le d_i,\; a_j = a_i \,\}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta$ thirty minutes, is how far the rest of the airport is running over its own
unimpeded baseline at this moment. Excluding $i$ from $W_i$ is what makes the feature
legitimate rather than a leak of its own label.

Because it measures the effect, $E$ carries causes that were never encoded: a closed
runway, an ATFM regulation, a blocked stand, a security incident. No feature naming those
causes exists in the model, and none is needed.
